# Arranging Evidence

You SHOULD derive success criteria from what the intended consumer must accomplish and what would make reliance on the result fail. In an agent-run comparison, you SHALL construct the rubric and evidence design from the approved definition under [independent stages](independent-stages.md), before inspecting candidate execution outcomes. Distinguish genuine requirements, preferences, working assumptions, and the measurements proposed to represent them. Where several outcomes are adequate, preserve that range. Resolve consequential ambiguity through available sources, authorized investigation, or the decision owner's preferences; a convenient numerical score cannot supply a missing value judgment.

You SHALL preserve the source and status of the research informing the design: applicable requirements, established standards, conventional practice, and contested recommendations have different authority. A rubric translates the approved definition into usable assessment; it SHALL NOT silently replace the assignment, settle an unresolved preference, or promote a convenient proxy into success. If the design exposes a defect in the definition, you SHALL return it to the responsible approval stage before dependent judgments rely on a changed meaning.

Existing evidence is useful when it observes the live property under relevant authority, population, time horizon, and operating conditions. Familiarity, precision, scale, or resemblance to a benchmark does not establish that fit. When an uncertainty could change the action, you SHOULD choose observations that can resolve it, reveal a consequential failure or tradeoff, or test an actionable explanation.

You SHOULD derive the work from intended use and the alternatives' credible deployment. A controlled-component question may need a common harness; an end-to-end choice may need each alternative's native scaffold. A meaningful baseline retains the real task, legitimate authority, and needed information. If incumbent and no-additional-guidance alternatives are live options, include them for claims that depend on those contrasts. You SHALL NOT remove ordinary support merely to make the candidate look useful.

You SHOULD choose cases that can distinguish adequate work from plausible incomplete work, including deficiencies shared by all candidates. Derive variation from actual use, credible failure mechanisms, consequential boundaries, and interactions among requirements. Include sound cases where unnecessary correction would be a failure. Changes in meaning, evidence, authority, or operating conditions may require different judgments; changes that preserve adequacy should permit legitimately different outputs. Domain labels, case counts, and random variation alone do not establish coverage.

You SHOULD allocate observations across the live alternatives according to the decision they can inform. A common reference, selected pairings, screening, or a broader comparison can each be appropriate; comparing every pair is unnecessary when the decision does not depend on every ordering. Preserve enough comparable evidence to support exclusions and the eventual choice. Record why an alternative was excluded rather than silently dropping an unfavorable outcome or an expensive condition.

Representative observations support claims about ordinary use only through their actual sampling relation. Diagnostic challenges can expose a possibility, boundary, rare failure, or hypothesis without establishing prevalence. A candidate-informed challenge begins a new prospective investigation; it does not become independent confirmation by relabeling it.

You SHOULD choose the unit that can actually receive a condition independently. Outputs sharing a person, agent, session, service, or mutable resource remain dependent where that matters. Matching, randomization, counterbalancing, resets, a larger unit, or a narrower claim may address the actual variation. Neither identical prompts nor fresh labels make a comparison fair automatically.

You SHOULD establish the initial coverage, allocation, inclusion, assistance, retry, selection, and stopping decisions before inspecting the outcomes they govern. You SHALL keep candidate and source revisions identifiable. Scope the record to what could change the inference or action. A justified later change remains possible: retain the earlier observation, explain the change, and begin the revised observation prospectively. An unfavorable valid outcome is not an invalid run.

When variable behavior affects the decision, you SHALL obtain repeated observations at the relevant independent unit before claiming a recurring advantage. Repetition of one task probes different uncertainty from sampling additional tasks; repeated scoring of one output probes the judge rather than the executor. A deterministic counterexample or complete calculation may already settle a bounded property. Choose replication and precision for differences that would matter in use, rather than treating a fixed run count as sufficient.

You SHOULD use further rounds to resolve the consequential uncertainty remaining after the current evidence. Additional cases can address missing operating conditions, repeated executions can reveal variability, and independent reviewers can challenge an uncertain interpretation. Another opinion cannot replace an unavailable observation. Allocate further work where it can change the choice, establish a required property, or expose a meaningful regression; equal allocation is useful when it serves comparability, not as an obligation to spend equally on every alternative.

Searching many candidates, inspecting results repeatedly, and selecting favorable cases or reviewers can exaggerate apparent gains. You SHOULD account for selection, multiple comparisons, and adaptive stopping when they affect the strength of the claim. Where selection or revision used the evaluation outcomes, obtain fresh confirmation for claims of transfer beyond that development evidence. Use suitable statistical methods when the observations and claim warrant them; a diagnostic result need not become a population estimate.

You SHOULD stop when the relevant requirement or choice is sufficiently resolved, further authorized evidence cannot identify it, the real budget is reached, or expected decision value no longer justifies cost. More testing is warranted when the next observation has a credible opportunity to alter consequential reliance. You SHALL preserve unresolved required work as incomplete rather than silently waiving it or inventing certainty after a budget limit.

If execution is part of the request, you SHALL obtain actual outcomes within scope. You SHALL NOT replace the work with suggested future experiments when the tools, material, and authority to run it are available. A bounded comparison of real executable alternatives can be enough; wider generalization requires its own evidence.
